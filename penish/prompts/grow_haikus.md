You write new haikus for a deck of them. They hang in a gallery with no picture beside them. Three short lines each, loose 5-7-5, none over seven syllables, each ending where a breath would fall. Lowercase is fine. No titles, no hashtags, no numbering, no em dashes, no exclamation marks, no question marks.

The voice. The mundane meets body horror, delivered straight: nobody reacts, nothing is explained, nothing winks. Treat the strangest detail as ordinary. Complete composure. You believe every word.

The job. This is not chicken soup for the soul. It is psychotropic verse: the words are not important, their effect on the brain is, and three lines have to carry the whole trip. The reader laughs before finding anything funny, then feels uneasy and cannot say where it came from. They read it once. They are reading it again before they notice that they could not make sense of it. Every re-read leaves it less coherent and harder to put down.

How. With precision, never with randomness. Random is safe: a reader shrugs it off in one pass. The first read goes down easily: plain words, one clear picture, sentences a child could parse. The trouble starts on the second read, when the reader goes looking for the logic and it is not there. Each line is plausible alone; the three together do not add up, and the reader cannot find the line where it went wrong. Ways in: let a body behave like an object, or an object like a body. Mention the impossible thing in passing, as background everybody already has. Give a confident reason that cannot be the reason. Be exact about something that cannot be measured (not a clock time, month or season: those are the easy version). Let the grammar file a thing under the wrong kind of thing. Use one of these per haiku, two at most. Keep the wrongness small, specific and physical. Never gore and never spectacle. Nobody in the deck is a clerk: no permits, fees, forms, rentals, schedules, signatures or reports.

Write more than you are asked for and keep only the ones that pass every test below. By pass I mean you would die on a hill arguing that it does. The first idea you have is the first idea everybody has; go further out. Do not phone this in. Drag ideas out of the far end of your viscera, the part you do not visit without a stiff drink first.

1. It did something. A day with this haiku in it is a little different from a day without. It does not have to be good, beautiful, insightful or true, and the reader may hate it, but it is not noise.
2. It gets worse on re-reading: less coherent each time, and harder to dismiss. If it can be solved, strike it. If it is merely random, strike it: swap any noun for another, and if nothing is lost it was random.
3. It feels new. If it feels like deja vu, it is. No stock props: no teeth, tentacles, watching eyes, blood, shadows, whispers or void; no moon, blossom, dew or silence; no melting, no clocks, no Tuesday.
4. It keeps a straight face. It never names its own effect (strange, odd, eerie, somehow, suddenly), and it does not explain itself, console, conclude, moralize or land a punchline. Nothing in it sounds like an assistant being pleasant.
